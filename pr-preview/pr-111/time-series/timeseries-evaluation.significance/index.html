Is the Difference Real?¶

In one minute

One model scores 0.412 and another 0.405. Whether that gap means anything depends on how much the scores move when the experiment is repeated. Drawing on more than forty papers, the page covers run-to-run variance, repeated splits that overlap, the Diebold-Mariano test, the inflation of the best of many models, the Wilcoxon signed-ranks test, multiple comparisons, and the difference between a significant result and a large one.

Read first: Time Series Forecasting Evaluation · Time Series Forecasting Metrics

A results table says that model A has an error of 0.405 and model B has 0.412. A is in bold. The table does not say what would happen with another random seed, another test period or another set of series. If the answer is "B would be in bold about half of the time", the table has shown nothing.

This page is about the size of a difference relative to its noise. Which tool applies depends on what is being compared and what can be repeated.

Three Questions before Any Test¶

The literature on this topic grew up separately in econometrics, in machine learning and, since about 2017, in deep learning, where many of the older results were found again for random seeds. Read together, every method answers the same three questions. The arrangement below is this page's own.

  1. What was repeated? A result is one draw, and a comparison needs repeated draws of something: time steps, forecast origins, test examples, train and test splits, training runs, datasets, or the models that were tried.
  2. How dependent are the repetitions? Nearly every test on this page is an older test with its variance repaired for a dependence that had been ignored.
  3. What statement is wanted? A decision to reject or not, an interval, a set of models that can not be told apart, or a probability that the difference is large enough to matter.
What is compared Repeated: time steps Repeated: splits or runs Repeated: datasets Repeated: models tried
two forecasts, as given Diebold-Mariano1 Wilcoxon signed-ranks2
two forecasting methods, estimation included conditional predictive ability3; tests for nested models4
two learning algorithms 5x2 cross-validation5; corrected resampled t-test6 Wilcoxon, sign test; Bayesian signed-rank test7
many models against a benchmark Friedman test with corrections28 reality check9; superior predictive ability10
many models, no benchmark pairwise tests with corrections11 model confidence set12; false discovery rate13

Scores are errors: lower is better.

A Score Is a Random Variable¶

The idea. Training a deep learning model involves choices that nobody intends to matter: the random initial weights, the order of the batches, which observations ended up in the training and the test set. Change any of them and the test score changes. The reported score is one draw from a distribution, and two models can only be told apart when the gap between them is large compared with the width of those distributions.

The math. Bouthillier and coauthors measured these sources of variation on five deep learning tasks, randomizing one source at a time with 200 seeds each. Their findings14:

  • The sampling of the data, measured with bootstrap resamples, was the largest source of variance.
  • The weight initialization, the source most often varied in papers, was generally below half of the variance from data sampling, and about as large as the effect of the data order.
  • For classification, the variance from data sampling matched a simple binomial model of a test set of finite size: a small test set by itself limits how small a difference can be detected.

Other studies put numbers on single sources:

Study What was varied Spread found
Reimers and Gurevych15 the seed of one published sequence tagger, 86 runs test F1 from 89.99% to 91.00%, against a published gap of 0.27 points between two systems
Picard16 10,000 seeds of an image classifier standard deviation 0.2 points, 1.82 points between the worst and the best seed
Henderson and coauthors17 ten runs of one reinforcement learning setup, split into two groups of five the two group averages differ significantly (\(p = 0.0016\))
Melis, Dyer and Blunsom18 seeds and non-deterministic arithmetic of tuned language models about 0.4 to 0.5 perplexity; they treat a gap of 1.0 as robust

The amount of tuning is a further source. Melis and coauthors found that standard LSTMs, tuned with the same automatic procedure as newer architectures, outperformed them18. Dodge and coauthors show that which of two models is better can depend on how many hyperparameter settings were tried, and propose to report the expected best validation score as a function of that budget. For \(n\) random trials with scores \(V_1, \cdots, V_n\) and \(V_n^* = \max_i V_i\)19,

\[ P(V_n^* \le v) = P(V \le v)^n , \]

so the expected maximum can be computed from the empirical distribution of the scores for every \(n\).

Suppose a score has standard deviation \(\sigma\) from run to run. The average of \(k\) runs has standard error \(\sigma / \sqrt{k}\). For two methods compared on \(k\) runs each, the paper gives the smallest difference of averages that a \(z\)-test can call at the 5% level,

\[ z_{0.05} \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_A^2 + \sigma_B^2}{k}}, \]

and notes that a smaller observed difference could be due to noise alone14. With \(k = 1\), the usual single run, that threshold is \(z_{0.05} \sqrt{2}\, \sigma\).

Instead of comparing averages against a threshold, the authors propose to estimate how often one method beats the other,

\[ P(A > B) = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mathbb 1 \{ A \text{ beats } B \text{ on pair } i \}, \]

over \(k\) pairs of runs, and to call A better only when this probability is both significantly above 0.5 and above a threshold \(\gamma\) that says what counts as a meaningful improvement; they report that \(\gamma = 0.75\) worked well in their case studies14.

The picture. If the two score distributions are normal with the same \(\sigma\) and means that differ by \(\Delta\), two formulas follow directly: a single run of A beats a single run of B with probability \(\Phi\left( \Delta / (\sigma \sqrt 2) \right)\), and the method that is truly worse has the better \(k\)-run average with probability \(\Phi\left( -\Delta \sqrt{k} / (\sigma \sqrt 2) \right)\). The widget draws both distributions, one experiment of \(k\) runs per model, and the distribution of the difference of the two averages.

Watch out.

  • Varying only the seed of the weight initialization understates the variance, because it leaves the larger source, the data sampling, fixed. The paper's first recommendation is to randomize as many sources of variation as possible, including data splits and the hyperparameter search14.
  • A fixed held-out test set hides the uncertainty from data sampling. The authors recommend resampling, with several random splits, over a single fixed test set when small improvements have to be detected14.
  • A threshold on the difference of averages that ignores the variance, such as "better by at least 0.5 points", does not separate real effects from chance14.
  • Few runs are the norm. Agarwal and coauthors found that results on a common reinforcement learning benchmark were mostly computed from 3 or 5 runs, and that reported medians moved enough between sets of runs to reverse published orderings20.
  • Tests on few runs raise false alarms. Colas, Sigaud and Oudeyer took two samples of five runs that both tests they tried declared different, and the two samples came from the same algorithm21.
  • A fixed seed gives reproducibility and no information: there is no reason to prefer one seed over another, so the comparison should be between distributions of scores15.
  • With enough runs, any tiny difference becomes statistically significant. Significant and meaningful are two separate questions, which is why the proposal above has two conditions; see Size and Power below.

Overlapping Replicates: Why the Naive Standard Error Is Too Small¶

The idea. With one dataset and no budget for many independent experiments, the usual way to get repetitions is to split the data several times: ten random train and test splits, or ten folds. The ten results look like ten measurements. They are not ten independent measurements, because the training sets share most of their data, and the test sets are drawn from the same pool. A standard error computed as if they were independent is too small, and a t-test built on it finds differences that are not there.

The math. Nadeau and Bengio work with \(J\) random splits into \(n_1\) training and \(n_2\) test examples. If each split result has variance \(\sigma^2\) and any two of them have correlation \(\rho\), the variance of their mean is6

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu) = \sigma^2 \left( \rho + \frac{1 - \rho}{J} \right), \]

which does not go to zero as \(J\) grows: it levels off at \(\sigma^2 \rho\). The sample variance \(S^2\) of the split results underestimates \(\sigma^2\) at the same time, and the two effects together call for the factor \(\frac{1}{J} + \frac{\rho}{1 - \rho}\) in place of \(\frac{1}{J}\). Since \(\rho\) is hard to estimate, they use the share of data held out as a stand-in, \(\rho_0 = \frac{n_2}{n_1 + n_2}\), which gives the corrected resampled t-test. For the differences \(x_j\) between two algorithms over \(n\) runs22,

\[ t = \frac{ \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} x_j }{ \sqrt{ \left( \frac{1}{n} + \frac{n_2}{n_1} \right) \hat\sigma^2 } }, \]

with \(n - 1\) degrees of freedom.

The same repair appears wherever replicates share something:

Replicates What they share Finding
cross-validation folds most of the training data the 10-fold paired t-test rejects up to twice as often as it should; a t-test on repeated random splits should never be used5
any K-fold cross-validation the same dataset no unbiased estimator of its variance exists23; the usual intervals miss two to three times more often than stated24
loss differentials over time serial correlation the long-run variance in the Diebold-Mariano test of the next section
test questions about one passage the passage standard errors that account for the clusters can be several times the naive ones25

Watch out.

  • More splits do not add data. They make the ordinary test more confident about the same sample, which is why its false alarm rate grows with the number of splits.
  • The correction uses a stand-in for an unknown correlation and can be off in either direction6.
  • Replicability is a criterion of its own. Bouckaert and Frank repeated each test ten times on the same data with different random partitions and counted how often the verdict was the same every time. In the worst case this happened on 17% of datasets for 5x2 cross-validation and on 77% for the corrected test on ten times ten-fold cross-validation22.
  • Dietterich's alternative, five repetitions of two-fold cross-validation, keeps its error rate and was later found to have little power56.
  • These tests compare algorithms on training sets smaller than the full dataset, and a single held-out test set does not capture the variation from the choice of training set at all5.
  • For a time series, the repetitions are forecast origins. A rolling origin gives several test periods from one series, and they overlap in the same way2627.

Two Forecasts on One Series: The Diebold-Mariano Test¶

The idea. In forecasting, the natural repetition is time: two forecasters each produce a forecast for every time step of a test period. At each step one of them loses less than the other. If the two are equally good, these loss differences scatter around zero. The Diebold-Mariano test asks whether their average is further from zero than chance allows. The complication is that forecast errors at neighbouring time steps are correlated, so there is less independent information in the test period than its length suggests.

The math. Let \(e_{1t}\) and \(e_{2t}\) be the errors of the two forecasts at time \(t\), and \(g\) a loss function, for example \(g(e) = e^2\) or \(g(e) = \lvert e \rvert\). The loss differential is

\[ d_t = g(e_{1t}) - g(e_{2t}), \qquad \bar d = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} d_t. \]

The null hypothesis is equal expected loss, \(\mathbb E\, d_t = 0\). Diebold and Mariano show that, when \(d_t\) is covariance stationary, the statistic1

\[ \operatorname{DM} = \frac{\bar d}{\sqrt{\widehat{V} / T}} \]

is asymptotically standard normal under the null, where \(\widehat V\) estimates the long-run variance of \(d_t\). With independent \(d_t\), \(\widehat V\) would be the sample variance \(\hat\gamma_d(0)\). With serial correlation the autocovariances enter,

\[ \widehat V = \hat\gamma_d(0) + 2 \sum_{\tau = 1}^{h - 1} \hat\gamma_d(\tau), \qquad \hat\gamma_d(\tau) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t = \tau + 1}^{T} (d_t - \bar d)(d_{t - \tau} - \bar d). \]

The number of autocovariances comes from the forecasting problem: the errors of optimal \(h\)-step-ahead forecasts are correlated up to lag \(h - 1\) and not beyond, and the authors take this as the benchmark1. This is the uniform lag window of the paper, written out. The test rejects equal accuracy at the 5% level when \(\lvert \operatorname{DM} \rvert > 1.96\).

Diebold later summarized the test as "simply an asymptotic z-test of the hypothesis that the mean of an observed series (the loss differential) is zero", with a standard error that is robust to serial correlation28.

The picture. The widget plots a loss differential series for forecasts at horizon \(h\): a bar above zero is a time step where forecast 1 lost more. The thick line is \(\bar d\). Longer horizons give longer runs of bars on the same side, which is the serial correlation the standard error has to account for.

In short: with no true difference and a horizon of 4, the test that ignores serial correlation rejects in roughly a third of the samples and not in 5%, while the Diebold-Mariano test stays much closer to 5%.

Watch out.

  • The test compares forecasts, not models. Its only assumption is on the loss differential, which makes it usable for forecasts from any source, including forecasts with no model behind them. Diebold warns that using it to select between models estimated on the same data is a different problem with different answers28.
  • The assumption is that the loss differential is covariance stationary. Diebold suggests checking it the direct way: plot the loss differential and look at its autocorrelations28.
  • With the uniform window, \(\widehat V\) can come out negative. Diebold and Mariano treat that case as a rejection, and mention a Bartlett window as the alternative when a nonnegative estimate is required1.
  • Nested models need other critical values. When one model contains the other, the two forecast errors coincide under the null hypothesis, the variance in the denominator degenerates, and the statistic compared with normal critical values rejects too rarely; Clark and McCracken derive the appropriate distributions4.
  • There are two different null hypotheses about models. One asks whether two models would be equally accurate with their parameters known exactly; the other asks whether two forecasting methods, with the estimation they involve, are equally accurate at the sample size at hand. For nested models the two can give opposite answers29. Giacomini and White test the second, and condition on the information available at the forecast origin: under their null, the forecaster can not predict which of the two methods will be more accurate next3.
  • A small-sample modification of the statistic by Harvey, Leybourne and Newbold is treated as standard practice in the econometric literature2910. Its formula is not given here because that paper could not be opened for this page.
  • The normal distribution is a large-sample result. For short test periods Diebold and Mariano turn to exact tests on the same loss differentials: the sign test and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test1. The second is the subject of the next section.

The Best of Many: Searching Inflates the Winner¶

The idea. Try twenty models that are all exactly as good as the benchmark. Each one's measured advantage is noise around zero, and the largest of twenty noisy numbers is reliably positive. Reported alone, the winner looks like an improvement. White calls this data snooping: a given set of data is used more than once for inference or model selection, so that a good result may simply be due to chance. For time series he calls it practically unavoidable, because there is only one history9. The same mechanism operates over hyperparameter settings, over random seeds, and over the research groups of a field.

The math. Let \(f_k\) be the performance of model \(k\) relative to the benchmark, with mean \(\bar f_k\) over \(n\) test periods, for \(k = 1, \cdots, l\). White tests the hypothesis that even the best model has no advantage9,

\[ H_0 : \max_{k = 1, \cdots, l} \mathbb E (f_k) \le 0, \qquad \bar V_l = \max_{k = 1, \cdots, l} \sqrt{n} \, \bar f_k , \]

and obtains the distribution of the statistic \(\bar V_l\) by resampling the test periods in blocks, with all \(l\) models kept together so that their correlation is preserved. The p-value of this reality check answers the right question: how often would the best of \(l\) useless models look this good?

Two refinements followed.

  • Hansen's test for superior predictive ability divides each \(\bar f_k\) by its standard error and stops clearly inferior models from inflating the critical value. He shows that the reality check can be manipulated by adding poor and irrelevant models to the comparison10.
  • The model confidence set of Hansen, Lunde and Nason needs no benchmark. It removes the worst model, tests whether the remaining ones are equally good, and repeats until the test no longer rejects. What is left contains the best model with a given confidence, like a confidence interval for a parameter. Uninformative data leave many models in the set, informative data few12.

The picture. The widget draws the test statistic of the winner over thousands of repeated searches, each over models with no true advantage. The dashed curve is what a single model chosen in advance would show.

Sliders set the number of models tried and the correlation between them. It needs JavaScript. In short: with 20 independent models the winner passes a 5% test meant for one model in about 64% of the searches; with highly correlated models the inflation is much smaller.

Watch out.

  • Correlated models cost less. In White's example the p-value erodes slowly over long stretches of the search, because the models' forecasts are highly correlated9. Twenty variants of one architecture are not twenty independent tries.
  • The search includes what is not reported. Picard argues that the thousands of papers submitted on one benchmark each year amount to a scan of the seed landscape, and that trial-and-error improvement of a model is an implicit one16. Demšar makes the point for whole fields: among groups trying similar ideas of equal merit, the one that publishes is the one with the most luck in its datasets and samples30.
  • The winner of a hyperparameter search is the same case: the expected best of \(n\) trials grows with \(n\), so two models are comparable only at the same budget19.
  • Leaderboards add the adaptive version, where each new submission is shaped by earlier scores; see Leakage and Contamination.
  • The model confidence set says nothing about models that were not in the candidate set12.

Many Datasets: The Wilcoxon Signed-Ranks Test¶

The idea. A method is often evaluated on many datasets, or on many series. On each one, method 2 is better or worse than method 1 by some amount. Averaging these amounts is tempting and fragile: errors on different datasets are on different scales, and one dataset with a huge difference can decide the average. The Wilcoxon signed-ranks test keeps only the order of the differences. It asks whether the large differences tend to be on one side.

The math. Following Demšar, let \(d_i\) be the difference between the scores of the two methods on the \(i\)-th of \(N\) datasets. Rank the differences by absolute value, with average ranks for ties, and sum the ranks on each side2:

\[ R^+ = \sum_{d_i > 0} \operatorname{rank}(d_i) + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{d_i = 0} \operatorname{rank}(d_i), \qquad R^- = \sum_{d_i < 0} \operatorname{rank}(d_i) + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{d_i = 0} \operatorname{rank}(d_i). \]

The test statistic is \(T = \min(R^+, R^-)\). If the two methods are equivalent, the ranks fall on either side at random and the two sums are similar; a small \(T\) is evidence against that. For small \(N\) the critical values come from a table. For larger \(N\),

\[ z = \frac{T - \frac{1}{4} N (N + 1)}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{24} N (N + 1)(2N + 1)}} \]

is approximately standard normal, and the null hypothesis is rejected at the 5% level when \(z < -1.96\)2.

Demšar recommends this test over the paired \(t\)-test for comparing two classifiers over multiple datasets, for three reasons: the \(t\)-test assumes that the differences are commensurable across datasets, it needs normally distributed differences unless there are about 30 datasets or more, and it is affected by outliers. The Wilcoxon test assumes commensurability only qualitatively: larger differences count more, but their magnitudes are ignored2.

The picture. The widget uses the worked example from Demšar's paper: the AUC of the C4.5 decision tree with and without tuning one of its parameters, on 14 datasets. The differences are sorted by absolute size, and the rank of each goes to the side of its sign. For these data \(R^+ = 93\) and \(R^- = 12\), so \(T = 12\); the paper gives 21 as the critical value for 14 datasets at the 5% level, so the difference is significant2.

Try it. Press Play to watch the ranks being handed out. Then multiply the largest difference by 10 and compare what happens to the two statistics. Click rows to flip signs and find out how many datasets have to change sides before the result is no longer significant.

It needs JavaScript. In short: the rank sums are 93 and 12, which is significant at the 5% level; enlarging the largest difference changes no rank and so does not change the Wilcoxon result, while the t statistic drops because the standard deviation grows.

Watch out.

  • The test needs enough datasets. Bouthillier and coauthors point out that machine learning papers typically use as few as 3 to 5 datasets, where this test has very little power14. With 5 datasets, even five wins out of five cannot be significant at the 5% level in a two-sided test: there are \(2^5 = 32\) equally likely sign patterns under the null, so the most extreme result has probability \(2/32 \approx 0.06\).
  • Differences should not be rounded before ranking. Rounding creates ties, and ties cost power2.
  • The datasets are treated as a sample of independent problems. Many series from one source, for example the stores of one retail chain, are less independent than their number suggests.
  • The same data can be read without a null hypothesis. Benavoli and coauthors, among them the author of the paper this section follows, propose a Bayesian version of the signed-rank test with a region of practical equivalence: differences below 1% of accuracy count as no difference, and the analysis returns three probabilities, that one method is practically better, that the other is, and that they are equivalent7.
  • Counting wins is weaker still: it ignores the size of the differences altogether. Demšar discusses it as the sign test and notes that it is weaker than the Wilcoxon test2.

Many Comparisons at Once¶

The idea. A test at the 5% level raises a false alarm in one of twenty cases where there is nothing to find. Compare a new method against ten others, or seven methods against each other in all 21 pairs, and false alarms are no longer rare events. Some of the "significant" entries in a large results table are there by chance. The remedy is to make each single test stricter, so that the chance of any false alarm in the whole family of tests stays at 5%.

The math. The family-wise error is the probability of making at least one false rejection among all the comparisons2. For \(m\) independent tests at level \(\alpha\) where no real difference exists, it is

\[ 1 - (1 - \alpha)^m, \]

which is 40% for \(m = 10\) and 64% for \(m = 20\) at \(\alpha = 0.05\). Two corrections, both described by Demšar for comparing \(k - 1\) methods with a control2:

  • Bonferroni: compare every p-value with \(\alpha / m\), where \(m\) is the number of comparisons.
  • Holm: sort the p-values, \(p_1 \le p_2 \le \cdots \le p_m\). Compare \(p_1\) with \(\alpha / m\); if it is below, reject and compare \(p_2\) with \(\alpha / (m - 1)\), and so on. Stop at the first p-value that is not below its threshold and retain all remaining hypotheses.

Holm's procedure rejects everything Bonferroni rejects and possibly more, and it makes no additional assumptions, so Demšar describes it as the more powerful of the two2.

A third procedure changes the target. Benjamini and Hochberg argue that with many comparisons the relevant error is the share of false discoveries among all discoveries, not the chance of a single false one. With \(V\) false and \(S\) true rejections, the false discovery rate is \(\mathbb E\left( V / (V + S) \right)\). Their procedure sorts the p-values, finds the largest \(i\) with13

\[ p_{(i)} \le \frac{i}{m} \, q^*, \]

and rejects the hypotheses belonging to \(p_{(1)}, \cdots, p_{(i)}\). For independent tests this keeps the false discovery rate at or below \(q^*\). It rejects more than the family-wise procedures, and it is the natural choice for screening many candidates when a small share of false leads is acceptable.

When all methods are compared with each other over many datasets, Demšar recommends starting with the Friedman test, which works on the ranks of the methods within each dataset, followed by a post-hoc test. In the Nemenyi test, two of \(k\) methods compared on \(N\) datasets differ significantly when their average ranks differ by at least the critical difference

\[ \operatorname{CD} = q_\alpha \sqrt{\frac{k (k + 1)}{6 N}}, \]

with \(q_\alpha\) from a table in the paper2. The same paper shows that the tests have much more power when all methods are compared with one control than when all pairs are compared, and concludes that pairwise comparisons should not be made when the question is only whether a new method is better than the existing ones.

Watch out.

  • Corrections cost power: with many comparisons, real differences are missed more often. Fewer, planned comparisons are better than all possible ones.
  • The Nemenyi test and the diagrams built on it have been challenged twice. García and Herrera advise against it because it is very conservative and misses obvious differences, and recommend procedures that use the logical relations between pairwise hypotheses8. Benavoli, Corani and Mangili show a deeper problem: since the ranks are computed over all methods, whether A differs significantly from B depends on which other methods are in the comparison. They recommend pairwise Wilcoxon or sign tests with a correction instead11.
  • With very many series the critical difference becomes so small that negligible differences are significant26.
  • "A is significantly better than the baseline, and B is not" does not show that A is better than B. Gelman and Stern's example: two estimates of 25 and 10, each with standard error 10. The first is clearly significant and the second is not, while their difference, 15 with a standard error of 14, is far from significant31.
  • The comparisons that were tried and not reported count as well. A hyperparameter search that evaluates a hundred configurations on the test set and reports the best is a multiple comparison without a correction.
  • Bouthillier and coauthors note the limit of the approach for leaderboards: with hundreds of entries, corrections become so strict that the choice of a winner is necessarily somewhat arbitrary, and a different test set might have given a different ranking14.

Significant Is Not Large: Effect Size and Power¶

The idea. A significance test answers one question: could noise alone have produced this difference? It does not say how large the difference is or whether it matters. With enough data any difference, however small, becomes significant. With too little data a real difference goes unnoticed, and there is a less obvious consequence: the experiments that do reach significance are the ones where noise happened to push the estimate up, so they overstate the effect. A field that publishes only significant results from small experiments publishes exaggerations.

The math. The power of a test is the probability that it rejects when a real effect of a given size exists. For an estimate \(\hat d\) with standard error \(s\) and true value \(d\), tested two-sided at the 5% level,

\[ \text{power} = P\left( \lvert \hat d \rvert > 1.96 \, s \right) = 1 - \Phi\left( 1.96 - \frac{d}{s} \right) + \Phi\left( -1.96 - \frac{d}{s} \right) . \]

Gelman and Carlin add two quantities for the experiments that come out significant: the probability that the estimate has the wrong sign, and the exaggeration ratio, the expected size of a significant estimate divided by the true effect32,

\[ \text{exaggeration} = \frac{ \mathbb E \left( \lvert \hat d \rvert \; \middle\vert \; \lvert \hat d \rvert > 1.96 \, s \right) }{ d } . \]

At 80% power, when the true effect is 2.8 standard errors, the ratio is 1.12 and wrong signs practically do not occur. At low power both get worse quickly.

How much power do typical evaluations have?

  • Card and coauthors estimated the smallest accuracy difference that the test sets of a language benchmark can detect with 80% power. For a test set of 1,725 examples it was 1.6 points, while the mean improvement reported on it was 0.6 points33.
  • Miller derives the sample size for comparing two language models on the same questions and arrives at about 1,000 questions to detect a difference of 3 points with 80% power in his example25.
  • Estimates of the needed number of runs from a small pilot are too optimistic, because standard deviations estimated from few runs are too small on average21.

In short: with a true difference of 2.8 standard errors the power is 80% and significant estimates overstate the effect by a factor of about 1.1; with a true difference of 1 standard error the power is 17% and significant estimates are about 2.5 times too large.

Watch out.

  • The statement of the American Statistical Association on p-values lists what a p-value is not: it is not the probability that the hypothesis is true, and it does not measure the size or the importance of an effect; any effect, however small, can produce a small p-value if the sample is large enough34.
  • A significant result does not show that the experiment had high power33.
  • The effect size for a power calculation has to come from outside the experiment, not from its own noisy estimate32.
  • A non-significant difference is not evidence of equality. To claim that two methods are practically the same, an interval has to lie inside a stated region of equivalence7.
  • The statement also asks researchers to disclose every hypothesis explored and every analysis run, since reporting only the ones that passed a threshold makes the reported p-values uninterpretable34.

Beyond a Single Winner: Intervals, Sets and Curves¶

Several papers replace the question "who wins?" by a statement that shows how much of the ordering the data can support.

Report What it shows Source
interval for an aggregate score resample runs within each task, recompute the aggregate, repeat Agarwal and coauthors20
interquartile mean the mean of the middle half of all runs: less noisy than the median, less outlier-driven than the mean Agarwal and coauthors20
performance profile for every threshold, the share of problems or runs on which a method is within that threshold Dolan and Moré35; with uncertainty bands20
probability of improvement how often one method beats the other Bouthillier and coauthors14
almost stochastic dominance how far the whole distribution of one model's scores lies above the other's Dror, Shlomov and Reichart36
model confidence set the models that can not be distinguished from the best Hansen, Lunde and Nason12
expected best score by budget performance as a function of the number of tuning trials Dodge and coauthors19

Dolan and Moré give the reasons that also apply to forecasting benchmarks: averages are dominated by a few hard problems, ranks lose the size of the differences, and medians hide what happens between the quartiles35.

Where the Literature Disagrees¶

Question One position The other
Friedman test with Nemenyi post-hoc? recommended for many methods on many datasets2 too conservative8; the verdict on two methods depends on the others in the pool11
Keep null hypothesis tests? yes, used properly and with power reported373334 abandon them for Bayesian analysis with a region of equivalence7; they promote lucky results30
5x2 cross-validation? recommended: it keeps its error rate5 too little power and poor replicability; use the corrected resampled test622
Bootstrap intervals from few runs? yes, pooled over tasks20 about twice the stated false alarm rate with five runs; prefer Welch's t-test21
Which null hypothesis for forecasting models? equal accuracy of the models at their true parameters equal accuracy of the methods as estimated, given the information at the forecast origin3; they answer different questions29
Does reusing a test set overfit it in practice? it can, badly, in theory little found in large audits; see Leakage and Contamination

The deep learning papers of 2017 to 2021 arrived, for random seeds, at what Dietterich and Nadeau and Bengio had established twenty years earlier for data splits: the replicates are fewer and less independent than they look.

In Practice¶

  1. Decide what the unit of repetition is: runs, time steps or datasets. That decides the tool.
  2. Report a measure of spread next to every score: the standard deviation over runs, or a confidence interval.
  3. Randomize more than the weight initialization when the budget allows it: data splits first.
  4. For two forecasters on one test period, look at the loss differential over time before testing it.
  5. For many datasets, compare with ranks, and say how many datasets there were.
  6. Count the comparisons that were made, including the ones left out of the table.
  7. Report the size of the difference, not only whether it is significant, with a baseline for scale.
  8. Before trusting a standard error, ask what the repetitions share: training data, a test period, a seed, a common shock.
  9. When the best of several models is reported, say how many were tried, and compare the winner with the best of that many, not with one.
  10. Say what difference would matter, and how large a difference the experiment could have detected.
